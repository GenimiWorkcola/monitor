【Title】A Democratic Congress won’t reset Beijing’s US strategy
【时间】Thu, 05 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
A domestic shake-up in Washington may alter the tone, but China sees a bipartisan consensus on containment that transcends party lines.
The US Capitol building in Washington DC, on November 3. Photo: Xinhua
The last time an American president lost both houses of the United States Congress in a midterm election was 20 years ago, under George W. Bush. Few were bold enough to predict the scale of the surge in support this time for Democratic Party candidates, particularly in the Senate, where they now hold a comfortable majority over the Republicans.
The result immediately raised the question of what it means for China-US relations, just two weeks before President Donald Trump is expected to attend the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation leaders’ summit in Shenzhen. A foreign ministry spokeswoman characterised the election as an internal matter for the US, and affirmed a commitment to advance bilateral ties and support the leadership consensus to improve communication, manage differences and expand collaboration.
The reality is that the election is likely to have some impact on bilateral relations. More than a victory for the Democrats, this was a crushing defeat for the Republicans. The results may well trigger fragmentation within the Republican Party.
As someone who rose from outside the party mainstream, Trump is an atypical but strong leader. His Maga (Make America Great Again) movement took the party by storm. Once in power, he marginalised many old-school Republicans, including former vice-president Dick Cheney and Bush. He also pushed aside the party’s more radical figures, such as former secretary of state Mike Pompeo, former national security adviser John Bolton and former ambassador to the United Nations Nikki Haley.
In the wake of this defeat, it remains to be seen whether China hawks within the Republican Party can stage a comeback.
The Democrats can be expected to scrutinise Trump’s controversial tariff policy more closely. They may also challenge his positions on Taiwan, artificial intelligence governance, critical minerals and technology restrictions.
Arguably, the Democratic surge was driven more by Republican failure than by Democratic ideas that seized voters’ imagination, amid broad frustration over the high cost of living, the war in Iran and trade conflicts with traditional allies such as Canada.
The Democrats’ focus will now be on addressing economic hardships and taming inflation – a major cause of the red collapse. They may put a brake on the war in the Middle East and make it harder for Trump to wage future wars. But they also face limitations on how much they can do over the next two years. Nevertheless, the US may end up with a lame-duck government and a more polarised society.
Nobody can predict how Trump is going to react to the defeat. Will he create more crises and double down on his policies, or will he retreat and withdraw?
According to analysts, the Democrats’ comprehensive victory could embolden Congress to assert greater authority over trade policy and scrutinise the deals Trump made with China, though a dramatic shift in Washington’s trade policy is unlikely.
On Washington’s approach to China, the general direction won’t change. Both Democrats and Republicans see China’s rise as an existential threat. They differ only in how they counter it. Democrats prefer to forge a coalition against China; this means roping in the European Union, the Five Eyes intelligence alliance between the US, the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, and the Quad security partnership. Republicans prioritise putting “America first”.
But these are finer differences, and an immediate shift is not expected. Besides, it is unlikely that a blue wave at the midterms alone could heal the distrust between the US and Europe.
Fundamentally, the result will not change Beijing’s policy. China’s stance is clear: it prefers cooperation over confrontation, but will not back down or compromise on national interests just to avoid a fight – particularly over issues like Taiwan.
At the end of the day, Trump’s handling of China has never been motivated by goodwill, but the practical realisation that China holds its own chokepoints over the US – particularly in critical minerals. So while some impact on the China-US relationship is expected after such a comprehensive domestic political reset, a major shift is unlikely.
By SCMP Editorial

【Title】The AI sell-off is a taste of things to come
【时间】Thu, 05 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
Companies with aggressive AI spending plans and negative free cash flow are being punished. Beneficiaries of AI spending might be next.
Google co-founder Sergey Brin speaks to US President Donald Trump during the Golden Age of American Innovation summit at the Institute of Peace on October 8. Shares of Google parent Alphabet had their worst day on October 29, after the company reported a second straight quarter of negative free cash flow. Photo: Getty Images via AFP
There are good reasons for investors to be bullish about artificial intelligence (AI). The earnings of technology firms in the S&amp;P 500 are expected to increase 54 per cent this year. For the index as a whole, profits will rise 33 per cent, turbocharged by the tech and energy sectors, data from FactSet shows.
In Asia, the impact of the AI boom is even more profound. Corporate earnings in South Korea and Taiwan, the two leading semiconductor hubs, are expected to grow a staggering 283 per cent and 73 per cent respectively this year.
There is even a silver lining in the recent turmoil in equity and corporate debt markets caused by worries over the financing of the AI infrastructure buildout. The sharp fall in the tech-heavy Nasdaq 100, which briefly entered correction territory on October 30, has helped drive down yields on US Treasury bonds as investors search for safe havens.
Yet a cursory glance at the scale and severity of the sell-off last month shows the AI spending spree has entered a new and more dangerous phase. Exceptionally strong profits and revenues from the United States’ “hyperscalers” – the leading tech companies and cloud service providers – are no longer a source of reassurance.
Mounting concern over the rapidly escalating buildout costs – hyperscalers’ combined capital spending is expected to increase 96 per cent this year to US$807 billion – and whether the investments will start to pay off in a meaningful way has put the economics and financing of AI under sharper scrutiny.
Anthony Saglimbene of Ameriprise Financial says the “AI math problem” has become more acute. The growth in tech firms’ capital expenditures far exceeds the increase in their revenues. “In our view, that gap can’t stay this wide indefinitely. Either revenue catches up more quickly, or spending plans get reset,” he said.
Construction equipment sits on land not yet developed near the Meta Prometheus Campus, an AI data centre and supercluster project, on November 1 in New Albany, Ohio. The AI spending spree has entered a new and more dangerous phase for tech giants including Meta. Photo: Getty Images via AFP
The problem, however, is that the risks in financing the boom – tech companies’ debt issuance has risen dramatically in the last two years as the spending spree eats into their free cash flow – are two-sided. Capital expenditure reductions would “raise questions about the durability of the investment cycle” while “further increases raise questions about financing, execution, and the eventual returns”, said Citadel Securities.
Once a rising tide that lifted all boats, the AI trade has become much more discriminating and volatile. Companies with aggressive capital expenditure plans and negative free cash flow are being punished.
A good example is Google parent Alphabet. On October 29, its shares plunged 13 per cent – the sharpest daily decline ever – after it reported a second straight quarter of negative free cash flow and revealed “AI mode” searches were eating into sales at its core advertising business. Microsoft’s share price, by contrast, has held up in part due to its positive free cash flow.
The growing pressure on tech companies to be more prudent in their capital spending while maintaining confidence in the sustainability of the AI boom leaves little margin for error at a time when other risks – higher interest rates and the backlash against the construction of data centres in several countries – are weighing on the AI trade.
This makes the timing of the stock market debut of Anthropic, the maker of the chatbot Claude, this month inopportune. The first company focused on frontier AI to go public, Anthropic plans to spend a staggering US$895 billion on computing power in the years ahead even though the growth in its revenues from AI services is already slowing, possibly due to growing competition from cheaper Chinese models.
[embedded media]
In a sign of the scale of the challenge confronting the leading tech companies, a report by Gartner last month found that over US$3.5 trillion of AI infrastructure spending could be “exposed to impairment” by 2030 because of insufficient revenues to cover financing costs and generate attractive returns.
What is clear is that AI’s mathematical problem will become more acute given the unprecedented surge in capital spending. While tougher curbs on data centre development – a near certainty in the US following the sweeping victory of the Democratic Party in the midterm elections – would make current AI infrastructure even more valuable, a sharp unwinding of the boom would wreak havoc on the global economy and markets.
Asia is the most exposed. The region’s tech sector – in particular the semiconductor industry in Taiwan, South Korea and Japan – has been the biggest beneficiary of the surge in AI spending, which “helped to insulate East Asian economies from what would have otherwise been a severe terms-of-trade shock”, said Carlyle.
Yet in Taiwan, whose economy grew a staggering 14.3 per cent in annualised terms last quarter, foreign outflows from the stock market reached a record high last month. The AI sell-off is no blip.

【Title】The US-China tech contest has moved – to the gatekeeping product testers
【时间】Thu, 05 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
The FCC ban on Chinese testing labs exposes a strategic weakness: Beijing does not control whether its test results are accepted by a major export market.
Workers produce electronic products for export at a workshop in the Yuexi Economic Development Zone, in China’s Anhui province on September 9. Photo: NurPhoto via Getty Images
The latest front in the US-China technology contest is not advanced chips, artificial intelligence (AI) or telecommunications equipment. It is the laboratories that certify whether an electronic product may enter the American market.
On October 29, the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) voted to stop recognising testing laboratories, telecoms certification bodies and accreditation organisations in China and in other economies that lack a “mutual recognition agreement” or comparable reciprocal arrangement with the United States.
The restrictions take effect on December 1, 2028. FCC chairman Brendan Carr cited a striking statistic: last year, about 82 per cent of US equipment-authorisation applications were associated with testing firms in China, while fewer than 4 per cent involved testing in the US.
The figure is politically powerful. It also explains why Washington is acting.
The strongest American argument is strategic dependence. Testing laboratories are an essential part of the electronics supply chain. Before a radio-frequency device can be marketed in the US, engineers must show it meets requirements concerning emissions, interference, exposure limits and other technical standards.
If most of this work is concentrated in one country, diplomatic confrontation, regulatory intervention or disruption to cross-border services could affect the approval pipeline for thousands of products.
US FCC chairman Brendan Carr speaking at a press conference in Washington on July 22. Photo: Reuters
The second concern is regulatory control. US authorities have investigated cases involving falsified test results, misleading statements about where technical work was performed, and certification bodies whose personnel and operations did not match their declared structure.
When laboratories, engineers and original records are outside US jurisdiction, investigation and enforcement become harder. Testing institutions also gain early access to unreleased products, engineering data and commercially sensitive information. Washington has reason to ask whether institutions in such a privileged position can be audited, investigated and held accountable.
The third argument is reciprocity. Chinese laboratories have been able to test products for the US market, while Washington says US laboratories have not received equivalent access in China. The FCC has now made reciprocal treatment a condition of participation in its equipment-authorisation system.
Industrial policy is the fourth motivation. Testing moved to China for much the same reasons electronics manufacturing did: lower costs, engineering expertise and proximity to factories and component suppliers. By excluding institutions located in China, Washington can redirect some of that business to American laboratories and approved partner economies.
The policy may help the US rebuild testing expertise and expand capacity. It also gives Washington leverage. Access to the American market can now be used to persuade other governments to accept reciprocal arrangements on US terms.
[embedded media]
National security, regulatory integrity, reciprocity and industrial policy are therefore embedded in the same measure. The FCC rule is an attempt to reclaim control over a market-access gateway that Washington believes has become dangerously concentrated abroad.
However, electronics manufacturers depend on rapid interaction among laboratories, designers, component suppliers and factories. Separating formal testing from production may require prototypes to cross borders repeatedly, technical questions to move between distant teams, and manufacturers to wait longer for retesting.
Large multinational companies can reserve overseas capacity and absorb the additional expense. Smaller exporters and companies will struggle more. Some may reduce the number of products they offer in the US; others may decide the market is no longer worth entering.
Washington should not assume that moving testing will lead to manufacturing reshoring. Companies can continue producing electronics in China while sending samples to recognised laboratories elsewhere.
[embedded media]
For China, the immediate losses will fall on testing institutions that have invested in equipment, engineers and FCC recognition. Larger groups may establish genuine operations abroad or acquire overseas laboratories. Smaller institutions may be forced to consolidate, withdraw from the US-facing business or close. The deeper damage is that China’s integrated design-manufacture-test-correct cycle may be weakened.
If final testing, certification coordination and customer relationships move abroad, that feedback loop becomes less efficient. Some high-value engineering, compliance and client-management functions may eventually follow the certification business overseas.
The strategic weakness exposed by the FCC decision is that China does not control whether its test results are accepted by a major export market.
[embedded media]
China can possess the manufacturing ecosystem, engineers and technical capacity, yet still lack the authority to convert those assets into internationally recognised compliance. The contest increasingly concerns who has the power to declare its product fit for sale. China must make its testing industry easier for outsiders to trust.
Transparent ownership, traceable data, independent audits and credible disciplinary procedures would make it harder to treat every Chinese laboratory as part of a single undifferentiated risk. The most effective answer to allegations of untrustworthiness is evidence that can withstand scrutiny.
The next phase of the US-China technology contest will not be fought only in factories, chip plants or research centres. It will also be fought at the gate where technical evidence becomes permission to sell. At that gate, credibility is power.
By Tan Poh Hwee

【Title】Understanding China’s October drills and the growing risk factor
【时间】Thu, 05 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
Each Chinese move may be calibrated, but its neighbours seem to be reading their accumulation as a trend, which can encourage the cooperation Beijing objects to.
Illustration: Henry Wong
Beijing closed five Chinese sea zones, from the Bohai to the South China Sea, for firing exercises from October 12 to 17, following Taiwanese leader William Lai Ching-te’s Double Tenth speech. None of the zones are close to Taiwan.
Across October, Beijing’s responses to what it regards as challenges have grown sharper, above all in its dealings with the Philippines. Yet the events show no clear shift towards pre-emptive action, and much of Beijing’s conduct is consistent with efforts to keep the external environment manageable.
The drills illustrate this. The first warnings were issued on October 8, before Lai spoke. Taiwanese defence minister Wellington Koo Li-hsiung assessed that the activities were for practice. Although Su Tzu-yun of Taiwan’s Institute for National Defence and Security Research saw the exercises as a “political statement”, timing alone does not indicate intent.
Beijing has not held full war games around Taiwan since late December, while Chinese President Xi Jinping and US President Donald Trump are set to meet again at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (Apec) summit in Shenzhen later this month. Scheduled firing is readiness, not a move to forestall anything.
Escalation has been clearest around the Scarborough Shoal, which Beijing calls Huangyan Island. The Southern Theatre Command of the People’s Liberation Army said in September it had stepped up readiness patrols there since August. On October 30, the Chinese military said it had further strengthened patrols since earlier that month and patrolled the waters and airspace of the shoal that day, as a “necessary countermeasure” against Philippine provocations.
Manila said a Chinese J-16 repeatedly closed in on a Philippine aircraft near the Scarborough Shoal on October 3, while Beijing contended that the Philippine aircraft had breached Chinese airspace. Each description presupposes whose jurisdiction applies.
[embedded media]
This has spilled over into diplomacy. On October 23, Philippine Defence Secretary Gilberto Teodoro warned that forcibly towing the BRP Sierra Madre off the Second Thomas Shoal (Ren’ai Jiao) would be “an act of war”, after a Chinese report that Beijing was weighing such a step. China then sent its defence attaché to the October 28-29 Asean Defence Ministers’ Meeting-Plus in Cebu; it was the lowest-level Chinese representation at the event since it began in 2010. Chinese Defence Minister Dong Jun had to attend a Communist Party meeting in Beijing.
China’s defence ministry later said the Philippines, as chair of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, had allowed a self-serving politician to obstruct its participation at Cebu. Teodoro replied that Beijing’s views had been heard and that no Asean member had raised similar concerns.
Given that China still attended, thereby reaffirming support for Asean centrality, its intent might have been to protest against the host, rather than withdraw from the mechanism.
The recent passage of the Liaoning aircraft carrier strike group shows how hard it is to read Chinese tea leaves. On October 22, the Philippine military detected the Chinese strike group about 50 nautical miles from shore, before it sailed southeast towards the Celebes Sea and then turned northeast. The South China Morning Post reported that the Liaoning could be positioning itself closer to the US-Japan Keen Sword exercise that the Philippines took part in for the first time.
A Philippine military spokesman read the closer-than-usual approach of the Liaoning as a show of presence; a Chinese expert told Global Times the Liaoning was on a routine training mission and taking a route less travelled that also allowed it to avoid typhoons. Both are plausible.
The USS Theodore Roosevelt leaves the Navy port of Yokosuka, near Tokyo, on October 19, to participate in the Keen Sword 27 exercise. Photo: Kyodo News via AP
With Japan, the sharpest change visible from recent events is in Beijing’s language. The Keen Sword exercise placed a US anti-ship missile system and a Japanese Type-25 missile on Yonaguni, 110km from Taiwan.
On October 29, the Chinese defence ministry called Japan a “real threat to regional security” and warned that any attempt to use Taiwan to contain China would carry an unbearable price. On October 20, the Chinese Southern Theatre Command Navy said it had tracked and monitored the USS Charleston near Sabina Shoal (Xianbin Jiao). It bears noting that this was a response after the fact, not an attempt to prevent the transit.
Around Taiwan, maritime records show intensity rather than a new threshold. On October 18, the Taiwanese coastguard said it had driven away 16 mainland Chinese vessels involved in operations around the main island and Pratas (Dongsha), and that it had conducted 11 operations this year against mainland Chinese coastguard vessels in Pratas waters. Beijing describes such patrols as being within its jurisdiction.
Taken together, the evidence suggests Beijing is responding more firmly where it sees its sovereignty claims tested, while keeping those responses bounded: firing exercise warnings were issued; low-level representation was chosen over a boycott; a warship was tracked rather than blocked.
Sovereignty commitments carry domestic weight. The Southern Theatre Command calls Huangyan Island China’s inherent territory, and the defence ministry has long held that the Taiwan question is an internal affair. Visible responses help keep those commitments credible, while bounded responses protect this month’s Apec summit and regional relations.
Intending to achieve stability is not the same as achieving it. At Cebu, both Teodoro and Shinjiro Koizumi, Japan’s defence minister, voiced concerns about China’s conduct in disputed waters.
While each Chinese response may be calibrated, its neighbours seem to be reading their accumulation as a trend, and that reading can encourage the cooperation Beijing objects to. More responses mean more encounters, and each makes routine readiness harder to distinguish from preparation. The risk lies less in a deliberate turn to pre-emption than in a gradual narrowing of the room where each side can read the other accurately.

【Title】As US-Iran war shows, China’s influence can’t secure its line to Gulf oil
【时间】Wed, 04 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
The conflict has made clear the limits of China’s efforts to cultivate commercial and diplomatic clout among its energy suppliers in the Middle East.
China’s Foreign Minister Wang Yi shaking hands with Saudi Arabia’s Foreign Minister Prince Faisal bin Farhan Al-Saud before a family photo for the attendees of a meeting of foreign ministers from Arab and Muslim-majority nations at the Diaoyutai State Guest House in Beijing on November 20, 2023. Photo: AFP
China is becoming increasingly important to the Gulf’s economic future. Yet the Gulf remains capable of disrupting China’s own.
That is the contradiction exposed by the US-Iran war. Beijing’s importance as an energy customer, investor and technology partner has grown, but its ability to guarantee the maritime routes connecting the Middle East to East Asia remains limited.
The next contest in global energy will concern not only who produces and consumes it, but who can preserve access when geopolitical conditions deteriorate.
The Strait of Hormuz remains contested. Yet energy still moves. Lloyd’s List Intelligence⁠ recorded at least 28 attacks or security incidents in October alone. Nevertheless, 104 non-Iranian-linked vessels transited the strait during October 5-11, 17 per cent fewer than the preceding week, but above August levels.
The 2026 global energy report by the Abu Dhabi International Petroleum Exhibition and Conference (ADIPEC), based on surveys of 1,832 senior energy decision-makers and 17 executive interviews, finds that 64 per cent believe the global system proved more resilient than expected. Some 77 per cent remain optimistic about the coming decade, while 72 per cent expect the Middle East to become more important to global energy.
The paradox is clear: industry leaders anticipate deeper integration precisely as the geopolitical foundations become less predictable. Resilience is not invulnerability.
[embedded media]
Alternative pipelines, strategic reserves and redirected cargoes have cushioned disruption, but at a cost. A chokepoint matters not only because it can halt trade, but because it can make continued access conditional, expensive and politically vulnerable.
For China, the problem has three dimensions. Commercially, Beijing has deepened Gulf relationships through long-term energy purchases, infrastructure investment and technology cooperation. These ties create leverage but cannot guarantee safe passage.
Diplomatically, China maintains relations with Iran, Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states. Yet access to rival capitals does not mean Beijing can reconcile their security demands or impose a settlement between Washington and Tehran.
Strategically, the United States remains central to Gulf maritime security. China’s economic exposure is growing faster than its ability to shape the military and political conditions governing access.
This does not make Beijing powerless. Renewables, nuclear generation, strategic reserves and diversified suppliers provide buffers. Chinese companies can divert cargoes or delay voyages. But adaptation is not control: changing a tanker’s itinerary does not remove the maritime insecurities.
[embedded media]
Nor is East Asia uniformly vulnerable. China combines heavy energy imports with substantial domestic production and technological capacity. Japan and South Korea depend more heavily on imported fuels and have fewer domestic alternatives.
The ADIPEC report’s country-level findings underline the contrast: 79 per cent of Chinese energy-sector respondents are optimistic about the coming decade, against 63 per cent in South Korea and only 23 per cent in Japan.
For Tokyo and Seoul, the immediate challenge is securing affordable energy while managing industrial costs and dependence on external security arrangements. Their room for manoeuvre is constrained by geography, domestic resources and alliance commitments.
China confronts a different problem. Its market size, financial resources and relationships across competing Gulf states give it a greater capacity to diversify supplies and influence commercial decisions. Yet these advantages cannot readily be converted into enforceable security guarantees.
[embedded media]
Renewed US-Iran hostilities could sharpen these differences. Three outcomes remain conceivable: limited diplomatic accommodation that restores more predictable access without settling the dispute; prolonged managed instability, with intermittent attacks and restrictions; or wider war targeting energy infrastructure and shipping.
The first would relieve immediate pressure without eliminating coercive leverage. The second would turn exceptional insurance, transport and security costs into routine commercial expenses. The third could overwhelm alternative routes and disrupt shipping and critical infrastructure.
The consequences would reach Asian manufacturing, petrochemicals, fertilisers and consumer prices. They would be amplified if the Red Sea and Strait of Hormuz faced disruption together: one insecure corridor cannot reliably substitute for another.
A screen grab taken from video footage released by the pro-Yemeni government Southern Giants Brigades on October 6 reportedly shows fighters near the Red Sea shore in the Dhobab district in Taiz province. Photo: Giants Brigades Media Centre / AFP
Energy security must therefore be understood differently. Contracts, reserves and diversified suppliers remain essential, but reliable access also depends on ports, pipelines, insurance, payment systems and maritime protection. Infrastructure has become an instrument of supply-chain statecraft.
The answer is not withdrawal from global markets. Interdependence can strengthen resilience if alternative suppliers and routes do not share the same vulnerabilities. Diversification that merely shifts dependence from one exposed corridor to another offers limited protection.
Beijing confronts a difficult choice. More Gulf investment increases commercial influence but also exposure. Greater diplomatic engagement may protect its interests while drawing it into disputes it cannot resolve. A larger military role could address some vulnerabilities but create friction with Washington and regional governments.
Japan and South Korea face related choices over reserves, alternative energy and external security arrangements. Gulf producers must maintain Asian demand while avoiding excessive dependence on any single power. The outcome is unlikely to be neatly divided geopolitical blocs; overlapping commercial and security relationships are more plausible.
The Gulf-Asia corridor is thus more than a trade route. It is where economic interdependence, strategic rivalry and vulnerability converge. The next test of power will not simply be who can secure energy supplies, but who can keep them moving when the international system comes under pressure.
By Marco Vicenzino

【Title】Can China tame its war machine?
【时间】Wed, 04 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
Outside attention has focused on the anti-corruption drive within the PLA. The purge of the military-industrial sector is just as striking.
Illustration: Mario Cameira
A popular Soviet joke goes like this: a factory worker was caught stealing parts, piece by piece, to assemble a sewing machine for his wife at home. When the interrogator asks whether he ever managed to put it together, the worker sighs: “No matter how many times I reassemble it, I keep ending up with a Kalashnikov [machine gun].”
The crushing dominance of the military-industrial complex, known in Russian as the VPK, was a decisive factor in the Soviet Union’s collapse. At its peak, it devoured roughly a quarter of Soviet gross domestic product. Even in its dying days, the USSR kept churning out a vast number of terrifying war machines but it could not, it seems, reliably manufacture a pen.
Mikhail Gorbachev’s own Soviet-made felt-tip pen famously failed to work when he was signing his presidency and the Soviet Union into history in 1991. He had to borrow a Montblanc from a CNN executive in the room to close one of the most inglorious chapters in modern Russian history.
Yet this structural failure is hardly unique to the Soviets. The United States may celebrate its Cold War victory as proof of systemic superiority, but in truth it is merely the pot less black than the shattered kettle beside it. Dwight D. Eisenhower, one of only three army generals elected president, famously warned of the same peril in his televised farewell address on January 17, 1961.
Eisenhower observed that the United States had built an unprecedented armaments industry alongside a military establishment numbering in the millions. Necessary though it was, he cautioned that this vast conjunction of private arms manufacturers and federal defence bureaucracy risked evolving into a self-serving political behemoth. The general-turned-statesman was concerned that this nexus would exert unchecked influence over economic, diplomatic and military policy, generating a perpetual incentive to inflate defence outlays and make the US more warlike.
Crucially, he also feared the rise of a scientific-technological elite whose research agendas would be dictated by defence contracts, shielding their projects behind specialised expertise that resisted civilian oversight.
Soviet Army tanks are parked near the Kremlin’s Spassky Gate in Moscow on August 19, 1991, after a coup toppled president Mikhail Gorbachev. Photo: AFP
Twenty years after that prescient warning, Gordon Adams published his seminal study, The Iron Triangle: The Politics of Defence Contracting. Using empirical data on campaign donations, corporate lobbying budgets, revolving-door appointments and congressional voting records, Adams showed that Eisenhower’s darkest projections had become reality.
Unlike the Soviet Union, the American economy expanded far faster than its military spending. The US military budget, even with Donald Trump’s jaw-dropping promise of US$1 trillion for this year, is about 3.4 per cent of GDP, compared with the average of 10 per cent during Eisenhower’s time.
The real danger of the American military-industrial complex lies instead in its propensity to produce aggressive foreign policy. According to data compiled by WarCosts, the United States has engaged in 251 foreign military conflicts since 1991, more than in all previous eras combined.
These lessons have not been lost on Beijing. When Deng Xiaoping launched the Four Modernisations in 1978, national defence was pointedly ranked last. Even today, as Xi Jinping pushes to realise that overarching modernisation programme, the central leadership remains cautious about runaway spending and keen to avoid the ruinous Soviet trajectory.
Yet China now confronts its own military-industrial dilemma. Modern defence technology has grown vastly more sophisticated than the simple mechanics of a Kalashnikov rifle, and closing the capability gap with Washington demands heavy reliance on the very scientific and technological technocrats Eisenhower described.
A protester holds a banner opposing the war with Iran during US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth’s opening remarks at a Senate Armed Services Committee hearing on US President Donald Trump’s budget request for the Department of Defence on Capitol Hill in Washington on April 30. The real danger of the American military-industrial complex lies in its propensity to produce aggressive foreign policy. Photo: Reuters
In 2007, at the 17th Party Congress, 13 officials with backgrounds in defence technology secured seats as full or alternate members of the Central Committee – the roughly 370-member inner circle of power. The calculation is mine.
By the 20th Party Congress in 2022, that figure had doubled to 26. Looking only at full Central Committee membership, the shift was even starker: rising from five to 11. Over four consecutive party congresses, technocrats steeped in defence and aerospace engineering have steadily risen through the political hierarchy. Amid intensifying geopolitical friction and acute technological competition with the West, Beijing has sought leaders with deep fluency in cutting-edge military hardware.
Yet inevitably, this rapid elevation has turned the defence sector into the latest battleground in China’s sweeping anti-corruption drive. Outside attention has focused on the sweeping anti-corruption campaign within the People’s Liberation Army, which has put about 20 senior military officials under investigation, including two vice-chairmen of the Central Military Commission. But the parallel cleansing inside the military-industrial sector is just as striking.
The most consequential case is Ma Xingrui, a Politburo member long seen as the vanguard of China’s aerospace technocracy. Ma is only the fourth sitting Politburo member to be investigated under Xi’s leadership, alongside Central Military Commission vice-chairmen He Weidong and Zhang Youxia. Former Chongqing party chief Sun Zhengcai, who fell a decade ago, was a civilian without any military connection.
[embedded media]
The trajectory of graft probes indicates where the leadership’s anxieties now concentrate. Fifteen years ago, high-level anti-corruption investigations rarely touched military contractors; today, the sector has become an epicentre of systemic scrutiny, particularly in connection with corruption within the PLA Rocket Force.
Since 2022, discipline inspectors have brought down three alternate Central Committee members with deep defence industry credentials: Zhang Zhongyang and Yuan Jie, former heads of China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation and China Aerospace Science and Industry Corporation respectively, and Jin Donghan, an accomplished ship engineer. In total, nearly two-fifths of the alternate Central Committee members drawn from the military-industrial apparatus have come under investigation. 【Title】Chinese scientists chill atoms to record low on Tiangong space station
【时间】Thu, 05 Nov 2026

【中文正文】
Scientists brought thousands of rubidium atoms to less than 10 trillionths of a degree above absolute zero.
The Tiangong Space Station was the base for a record-breaking experiment on rubidium atoms. Photo: CMSA
Chinese scientists have chilled atoms aboard the Tiangong space station to the lowest temperature ever measured, smashing US and German records and paving the way for ultra-precise space sensors that could find hidden minerals, track climate change and test Einstein’s theory of gravity.
Researchers from Peking University and the Chinese Academy of Sciences brought thousands of rubidium atoms, packed into a cloud smaller than a grain of sand, to less than 10 trillionths of a degree above absolute zero. The results were published in the journal Science on Friday.
That is about four times colder than the previous best, set in a German drop tower, and colder than anything reached by Nasa’s Cold Atom Lab on the International Space Station (ISS). The cloud held together for seven seconds, much longer than the two seconds achieved in the drop tower and about one second aboard the ISS.
Nick Bigelow, a physicist at the University of Rochester who has led experiments on Nasa’s lab, called the findings “wonderful results”. He said his own team has since cooled atoms to about 15 trillionths of a degree, in work not yet published, so the Chinese result still comes out on top.
Ernst Rasel of Leibniz University Hannover, whose group set the earlier mark, said the experiment led by Chen Xuzong of Peking University “sets a new record” for how cold and how long a quantum gas can be observed in free fall.
He said the gas would be “a formidable source” for quantum sensors “with plenty of applications in fundamental physics and Earth observation”.
Scientists have spent decades racing towards absolute zero – the bottom of the temperature scale – because the colder atoms get, the slower they move and the easier they are to study.
Their first big tool was beams of light, fired from all sides to slow the particles down and hold them in place.
[embedded media]
Chill them even further and something strange happens: the atoms stop acting alone and move in perfect lockstep, like one giant “super atom” known as a Bose-Einstein condensate. First made in 1995 in a Nobel-winning feat, this exotic form works like a magnifying glass on the quantum world.
But on Earth, gravity gets in the way. It drags the ultracold atoms down, forcing researchers to grip them tightly and cutting short how long they can be watched.
Drop towers and rockets buy only a few seconds of free fall, but in orbit the atoms can float freely, making space the ideal lab for such experiments.
On the Tiangong, the Chinese team first cooled about 2 million rubidium atoms in a tight laser trap until they formed a condensate. They then shifted the cloud into a wider trap and dimmed the light, letting the particles slow almost to a standstill.
Unlike the German and Nasa experiments, which rely on magnets, the Chinese set-up holds the atoms with lasers alone.
That means it can work with a wider range of elements, opening the door to the world’s most accurate clocks and new detectors for ripples in space-time, according to Rasel.
Only a small fraction of the atoms survived the cooling process, but a laser trap with an adjustable size could boost the count in future runs, the researchers wrote. That mattered because quantum sensors worked best with large numbers of atoms, he said.
Rasel’s group wants to turn such ultracold atoms into gravity sensors that would run far longer in orbit, and hopes to test the idea soon on a new instrument bound for the ISS.
Bigelow said the next goal should be to drop two different kinds of atoms side by side to see whether they fell at exactly the same rate, a quantum version of Galileo’s legendary experiment of dropping two balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa.

China’s emissions-reduction commitments were also boosting the resource-recycling industry, and government trade-in programmes designed to encourage consumption were making the recycling system increasingly important, they added.

“Against the backdrop of intensifying geopolitical rivalry and rising resource protectionism, strengthening domestic resource security has become a practical imperative for safeguarding industrial and supply chain security,” it said, urging the tapping of discarded mobile phones, computers, vehicles that had reached the end of their lives and old household appliances for additional resources.
Last year, China recycled over 230 million waste electrical and electronic products, dismantled 10.46 million vehicles and reused 400,000 tonnes (441,000 short tons) of batteries, according to a report from the China National Resources Recycling Association in April charting growth in the sector.
Overall, it recycled 417 million tonnes (460 million short tons) of renewable resources last year, up 4.1 per cent year on year and marking a third consecutive year of growth, the association said.
However, Li, the Shanghai scrap collector, said growth would be higher if consumers were more willing to spend.
“Overall consumption has remained weak this year,” he said. “People generally just keep reusing household items as long as they still work, so there isn’t as much recyclable waste to collect as you might imagine.”
With people remaining concerned about the state of the economy and their future incomes, government figures show the trade-in programme has been losing momentum.
Home-appliance replacements in the first nine months were down about 20 per cent year on year, and overall sales of goods under the programme in the first half of the year fell to 1.1 trillion yuan, from 1.6 trillion yuan in the same period for 2025.
Individuals and businesses had swarmed into the recycling sector amid the recent price increases, Aifenlei’s Xu said, but most were small players.
“Most of the newcomers are operating without licences, occupying public spaces, piling things up wherever they like and polluting the environment,” she said. “There’s really no difference between them and the waste recycling stations of more than 20 years ago.”
[embedded media]
As a result, Xu said she viewed the short-term boom as a bad thing because it created urban management challenges, disrupted market order and posed safety hazards.
But, she added, the influx of people joining the sector was good for the industry in the long run.
“The fact that capital and labour are both willing to come in means the industry has value and prospects,” Xu said. “Also, I think it can force the industry to become more standardised – with more people and intensifying competition, the government will have no choice but to regulate it.”
Zhang Miao, the founder of Shanghai Ecocycle, a think tank focused on the circular economy, said that a manufacturing giant like China had huge demand for raw materials, and recycling waste materials was an important supplement to its supply chain.
“In the meantime, because China’s manufacturing sector is extremely comprehensive, the recycling industry that supports it is equally comprehensive,” she said. “As a result, China is very strong in terms of both the completeness of the industrial chain and its technological level.”
But she called on the industry to improve sorting at the source to produce better quality recycled materials.
“If you don’t sort at the source, say by picking bottles out of mixed or kitchen waste, the recycled bottles will be full of contaminants,” she said.
The Institute of Public and Environmental Affairs, a Beijing-based NGO, said in a report released in January that while leading cities such as Suzhou and Shanghai had built “world-class sorting systems”, voluntary waste sorting was not yet common in most Chinese cities.
A city waste-sorting index compiled by the institute gave Suzhou, in eastern China’s Jiangsu province, a score of 96 out of 100 last year and neighbouring Shanghai a score of 84. But the average score of the 104 cities it assessed was just 18.04.

【Title】PBOC pledges support for stocks as China’s market slide defies rescue efforts
【时间】Tue, 03 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
Central bank governor signals further support for capital market and property sector after recent measures fail to stem losses.
The PBOC’s moves come after a joint meeting at which several ministry-level agencies pledged to boost market liquidity, restore investor confidence and support the A-share market. Photo: Getty Images
China’s central bank governor has reaffirmed support for the country’s beleaguered stock market after a series of rescue measures failed to put a floor under plummeting share prices.
Pan Gongsheng, governor of the People’s Bank of China (PBOC), pledged in a work report presented to the standing committee of the National People’s Congress on Tuesday that more actions would be taken to ease financial risks in key areas, including the capital market and the property sector.
“Monetary policy tools will be effectively implemented to buoy the capital market,” Pan said in the report, according to state news agency Xinhua. “To maintain a smooth operation of the real estate industry, a ‘white list’ mechanism will be set up.”
His statement was published after the market close on Tuesday, when major A-share indicators extended losses despite recent efforts by authorities, including the PBOC and the China Securities Regulatory Commission, to shore up investor sentiment.
On Monday, the PBOC announced the launch of a new one-year swap facility allowing institutional investors to exchange illiquid assets for liquid ones with the central bank to buy stocks.
It also slashed the interest rate on a relending programme that supports share buy-backs by a quarter of a percentage point to 1 per cent.
Investors are expecting authorities to roll out stronger incentives to stem the market slideShen Yi, Shanghai-based fund manager
The moves followed a joint meeting in Beijing on Monday at which several ministry-level agencies pledged to boost market liquidity, restore investor confidence and support the A-share market.
The Shanghai Composite Index slid 0.8 per cent to 3,608.10 on Tuesday, while the Star Market 50 Index tracking the country’s technology stars plunged 3.8 per cent to 1,151.28.
“As the central bank governor further voices support for the stock market, investors are expecting authorities to roll out stronger incentives to stem the market slide,” said Shen Yi, a Shanghai-based fund manager. “The message is clear that top policymakers are trying to stabilise the market.”
The Shanghai composite gauge lost 9.7 per cent from September to October amid mounting worries about an uneven ­economic recovery and an unwinding of artificial intel­ligence bets.
The CSI 300 Index, which tracks major firms listed on both the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges, tumbled 8.5 per cent in October, its worst monthly performance in a decade.
Chinese regulators have historically stepped in during periods of sharp market volatility, as steep declines can inflict heavy losses on millions of retail investors and risk undermining market confidence.

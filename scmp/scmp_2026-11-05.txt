【Title】DeepSeek engineer defends push for open-source AGI despite higher risks
【时间】Wed, 04 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
Some AI pioneers have warned that technology smarter than humans may escape human control.
DeepSeek engineer Gao Xi speaks on an AI panel at the World Internet Conference on Tuesday. Photo: kankanews
DeepSeek is committed to open-source artificial intelligence to accelerate the arrival of artificial general intelligence (AGI), even if it heightens safety risks, a company engineer says, defying growing safety concerns from the US and scrutiny in China over whether the strategy poses national security risks.
Speaking on Tuesday on an AI panel at the World Internet Conference (WIC) in Wuzhen, DeepSeek engineer Gao Xi said open-source development promoted shared innovations and technical breakthroughs, framing the technology’s rapid progress as a race against the clock, rather than against rival firms.
“If AGI is going to arrive in 10 years, then I hope we can bring this forward to one year. If it is going to arrive in one year, then I hope it can arrive tomorrow,” Gao said, in rare remarks from a DeepSeek representative.
“AI is developing faster every year. For AI model companies, the real competitor in the AI race is not other companies, but time,” Gao said. He added that technical barriers erected by competitors would only temporarily slow competition, not stop the inevitable arrival of AGI.
Gao’s remarks come as China’s open-source push emerges as a key flashpoint in the US-China tech rivalry. Unlike closed-weight systems championed by American front-runners such as OpenAI and Anthropic, open-weight models allow anyone with sufficient hardware to download and modify the code without vendor oversight.
The opening ceremony of the 2026 World Internet Conference in Wuzhen, in China’s eastern Zhejiang province. Photo: Xinhua
While Beijing has supported open-source ecosystems as a viable way to counter US tech containment, security experts have cautioned that releasing model weights could lower the barrier for rogue actors to conduct cyberattacks or develop bioweapons.
At the same time, some AI pioneers – including Turing Award winners Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio – have warned that AGI may escape human control.
Acknowledging that open-source releases “may amplify the impact of safety issues”, Gao said DeepSeek was working to strengthen safety features, including classifications for its models, though he did not provide specific details.
The “irreversible” nature of open-weight models presented additional safety challenges, acknowledged Liu Dayiheng, project lead for Alibaba Group Holding’s open-weight Qwen models, who also appeared on the panel.
However, Liu pushed back against the closed ecosystem model favoured in the West, arguing that the solution lay in a comprehensive governance regime and industry standards rather than technological gatekeeping.
“Without open source, the core technologies of large language models would only be in the hands of a few companies,” Liu said. “Open source allows developers around the world to contribute technology and innovate together.”
Alibaba owns the South China Morning Post.
[embedded media]
Beijing is balancing both sides. While authorities proposed new export controls last month targeting frontier AI models with advanced cyber-offensive capabilities, top officials maintained support for open-source software.
Both DeepSeek and Shanghai-based MiniMax completed domestic registrations to open-source their latest models, state media reported last month.
This year’s WIC, convened under the theme “Open Source and Openness”, drew around 1,800 delegates from 138 countries and regions, according to official figures.
Addressing the opening ceremony on Tuesday, Premier Li Qiang said China would continue to pursue “open innovation, promote technology transfer ... and work with all to build an open, inclusive and dynamic global ecosystem for digital innovation”.
The AI panel was moderated by Wang Jian, an academician at the Chinese Academy of Engineering and chairman of the WIC’s AI committee, as well as founder of Alibaba Cloud. Other panellists included Wen Feng, chief architect and head of open platform at MiniMax, and John Higgins, chairman of the International AI Governance Association.
At a separate WIC forum on Wednesday, Unitree Robotics founder Wang Xingxing said much of the data collected by robots remained underused, adding that self-improving physical AI models could help make better use of the data to accelerate technological advances.

It was not ‌immediately clear how close the telescope’s re-entry point was to North Sumatra’s capital and largest city, Medan, home to about 2.5 million people. The population of the province as a whole totals around 16 million.

Nasa’s prized US$500 million Swift satellite was primarily designed to study ⁠gamma-ray bursts – the most intense blasts of energy in the universe – using its multi-wavelength array of three telescopes.

For Finland and Norway, direct contact with Russia is not new, although the two countries have different experiences of managing the relationship. Finland, which joined Nato in 2023, has taken a firm position towards Russia while leaving open the possibility of future engagement. Finland shares a long land border, making changes in Russian military deployments and regional security arrangements especially relevant to its defence planning. Norway faces different challenges in the High North, where military activity, maritime security and cross-border relations remain important.
A peace settlement in Ukraine might end the fighting, but difficult questions about Russia’s military presence and Nato’s security arrangements in northern Europe will remain. Finland and Norway will have to deal with these issues long after negotiations end.

Finland and Norway emphasised that they had consulted Ukraine before the call and were speaking on behalf of their own governments, not Europe as a whole. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky subsequently welcomed their diplomatic support and discussed preparations for further negotiations.

European countries will have to live with the security consequences of any agreement, even if they are not directly involved in determining its terms. For Russia’s neighbours, direct diplomatic contact provides an additional opportunity to express concerns that might not receive equal attention in broader negotiations.

Countries bordering Russia might have particular reasons to maintain direct contacts, but such initiatives require consultation with relevant partners and clarity about whose interests they represent.
The October 27 call produced no publicly announced breakthrough, but it nevertheless shows how Russia’s neighbours can seek a direct role in diplomacy as negotiations over Ukraine evolve. Finland and Norway will have to manage the security consequences of any settlement long after the talks themselves have ended.
Europe’s task is to make room for those concerns while ensuring national initiatives support a coherent diplomatic effort. Unity over Russia does not require identical channels of communication.

【Title】The US-China tech contest has moved – to the gatekeeping product testers
【时间】Thu, 05 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
The FCC ban on Chinese testing labs exposes a strategic weakness: Beijing does not control whether its test results are accepted by a major export market.
Workers produce electronic products for export at a workshop in the Yuexi Economic Development Zone, in China’s Anhui province on September 9. Photo: NurPhoto via Getty Images
The latest front in the US-China technology contest is not advanced chips, artificial intelligence (AI) or telecommunications equipment. It is the laboratories that certify whether an electronic product may enter the American market.
On October 29, the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) voted to stop recognising testing laboratories, telecoms certification bodies and accreditation organisations in China and in other economies that lack a “mutual recognition agreement” or comparable reciprocal arrangement with the United States.
The restrictions take effect on December 1, 2028. FCC chairman Brendan Carr cited a striking statistic: last year, about 82 per cent of US equipment-authorisation applications were associated with testing firms in China, while fewer than 4 per cent involved testing in the US.
The figure is politically powerful. It also explains why Washington is acting.
The strongest American argument is strategic dependence. Testing laboratories are an essential part of the electronics supply chain. Before a radio-frequency device can be marketed in the US, engineers must show it meets requirements concerning emissions, interference, exposure limits and other technical standards.
If most of this work is concentrated in one country, diplomatic confrontation, regulatory intervention or disruption to cross-border services could affect the approval pipeline for thousands of products.
US FCC chairman Brendan Carr speaking at a press conference in Washington on July 22. Photo: Reuters
The second concern is regulatory control. US authorities have investigated cases involving falsified test results, misleading statements about where technical work was performed, and certification bodies whose personnel and operations did not match their declared structure.
When laboratories, engineers and original records are outside US jurisdiction, investigation and enforcement become harder. Testing institutions also gain early access to unreleased products, engineering data and commercially sensitive information. Washington has reason to ask whether institutions in such a privileged position can be audited, investigated and held accountable.
The third argument is reciprocity. Chinese laboratories have been able to test products for the US market, while Washington says US laboratories have not received equivalent access in China. The FCC has now made reciprocal treatment a condition of participation in its equipment-authorisation system.
Industrial policy is the fourth motivation. Testing moved to China for much the same reasons electronics manufacturing did: lower costs, engineering expertise and proximity to factories and component suppliers. By excluding institutions located in China, Washington can redirect some of that business to American laboratories and approved partner economies.
The policy may help the US rebuild testing expertise and expand capacity. It also gives Washington leverage. Access to the American market can now be used to persuade other governments to accept reciprocal arrangements on US terms.
[embedded media]
National security, regulatory integrity, reciprocity and industrial policy are therefore embedded in the same measure. The FCC rule is an attempt to reclaim control over a market-access gateway that Washington believes has become dangerously concentrated abroad.
However, electronics manufacturers depend on rapid interaction among laboratories, designers, component suppliers and factories. Separating formal testing from production may require prototypes to cross borders repeatedly, technical questions to move between distant teams, and manufacturers to wait longer for retesting.
Large multinational companies can reserve overseas capacity and absorb the additional expense. Smaller exporters and companies will struggle more. Some may reduce the number of products they offer in the US; others may decide the market is no longer worth entering.
Washington should not assume that moving testing will lead to manufacturing reshoring. Companies can continue producing electronics in China while sending samples to recognised laboratories elsewhere.
[embedded media]
For China, the immediate losses will fall on testing institutions that have invested in equipment, engineers and FCC recognition. Larger groups may establish genuine operations abroad or acquire overseas laboratories. Smaller institutions may be forced to consolidate, withdraw from the US-facing business or close. The deeper damage is that China’s integrated design-manufacture-test-correct cycle may be weakened.
If final testing, certification coordination and customer relationships move abroad, that feedback loop becomes less efficient. Some high-value engineering, compliance and client-management functions may eventually follow the certification business overseas.
The strategic weakness exposed by the FCC decision is that China does not control whether its test results are accepted by a major export market.
[embedded media]
China can possess the manufacturing ecosystem, engineers and technical capacity, yet still lack the authority to convert those assets into internationally recognised compliance. The contest increasingly concerns who has the power to declare its product fit for sale. China must make its testing industry easier for outsiders to trust.
Transparent ownership, traceable data, independent audits and credible disciplinary procedures would make it harder to treat every Chinese laboratory as part of a single undifferentiated risk. The most effective answer to allegations of untrustworthiness is evidence that can withstand scrutiny.
The next phase of the US-China technology contest will not be fought only in factories, chip plants or research centres. It will also be fought at the gate where technical evidence becomes permission to sell. At that gate, credibility is power.

Thaipusam is a major annual festival observed by Tamil Hindus honouring Lord Murugan, during which devotees perform acts of penance, including bodily piercings and carrying ornate devotional offerings known as kavadi.
Aliff said he had fasted on water and vegetables for a week before joining the rituals in January 2024. “I acknowledge my negligence and failure in fulfilling my responsibility as a mother,” she said. Aliff’s younger sister also took part in the ceremony, expressing regret for not offering him better advice.
Their declarations were signed and endorsed by senior Islamic officials, including the Mufti of Selangor, along with two other witnesses.
Faith on trial
Islam is Malaysia’s official religion, adhered to by nearly two-thirds of the population. Under the country’s dual legal system, state-level sharia courts hold jurisdiction over Muslims in personal, family and religious matters. While the nation’s sizeable Buddhist, Christian and Hindu minorities freely practise their faiths, ethnic Malays – defined under the federal constitution as Muslim – face strict restrictions on acts deemed contrary to Islamic teachings.

“It’s simply a show of power by the religious authorities,” Ahmad Fauzi said. “It serves no theological purpose.”

Researchers use a device mounted on the outside of the Tiangong space station to make millimetre-scale cuts.
Precision laser cutting involves focusing a beam of light to slice or melt material with extreme accuracy. Photo: Shutterstock
China’s space scientists have become the first to carry out precision laser cutting in orbit, slicing a piece of “large-scale equipment” with a robotic arm fixed to the outside the Tiangong space station.
The official Science and Technology Daily reported on Wednesday that millimetre-scale cuts were made by a laser maintenance device operated remotely from “hundreds of kilometres away”.
Precision laser cutting involves focusing a beam of light to slice or melt material with extreme accuracy, a difficult and risky process in orbit.
The Tiangong device was developed jointly by Huazhong University of Science and Technology (HUST) and the Shanghai Institute of Space Power-Sources, with the researchers taking just a year to go from initial design to a flight-ready model.
Professor Huang Yu, who led the HUST research team, told Science and Technology Daily that regular maintenance and high-precision repair were vital to the long-term operation of space stations.
To translate ground-based laser processing equipment into space, the team overcame several challenges, including operating in the extremes of space, achieving millimetre-level positioning and executing micrometre-level precision cutting, Huang said.
According to the report, the Tiangong device operates using multi-axis positioning mechanisms and is guided by an advanced vision system.
“A multilevel vision system guides, identifies and positions the device, automatically completing high-precision laser cutting and waste material recovery,” HUST’s mechanical science and engineering school said in a social media post in September.
The school said automated repairs could now be completed on the Tiangong, which would help to extend its lifespan and lay the foundations for an in-orbit maintenance system.
HUST has been identified as a Chinese institution of concern by the United States, with companies and facilities affiliated with the university appearing on the US Bureau of Industry and Security Entity List.
The cuts were carried out by a laser maintenance device mounted to a robotic arm outside China’s Tiangong space station. Image: HUST/Science and Technology Daily
Space-based laser technologies have drawn scrutiny over dual-use concerns, particularly their potential to damage, disable or destroy other spacecraft.
Both Beijing and Washington have pursued directed-energy and laser-weapon research, fuelling fears of an orbital arms race as each seeks to gain a strategic edge in space.
Researchers are grappling with how long-term orbital and lunar operations can be made more efficient and cost-effective.
Nasa is working on in-space servicing and manufacturing for Earth orbit and the moon, including laser-beam welding.
“Laser systems can perform joining, cleaning, cutting and repair activities, which will enable the construction of large in-space structures that could not fit on a single launch vehicle,” according to an abstract by the Marshall Space Flight Centre posted on Nasa’s STI research repository in July.
The abstract said Nasa was working on a number of space laser welding projects, including a compact vacuum welding chamber – which is planned to launch to the International Space Station – and an autonomous robotic arm to perform welds in a simulated lunar surface environment.
In a report on space welding published in 2020, Nasa said that welding and additive manufacturing in space could help repair spacecraft more economically without requiring new parts to be shipped from Earth.
As countries aim to build long-term presences in space and launch more spacecraft into Earth orbit and beyond, concerns have also been raised about the potential weaponisation of spacecraft.
Last month, a report by the US Defence Intelligence Agency alleged that China could deploy space-based directed-energy weapons by the late 2020s, though it did not present evidence for the forecast or say whether this would be a laser- or high-power microwave-based device.

【Title】Lithuania builds world’s biggest ultra-fast laser factory amid China tensions
【时间】Thu, 05 Nov 2026
----------------------------------------
【中文正文】
Ties between Beijing and Vilnius remain strained over Taiwan, but Litilit CEO says supply chain realities can override political posturing.
Litilit’s new factory in Vilnius, Lithuania will be able to produce 3,000 femtosecond lasers per year. Photo: Litilit
Amid tensions with China, a small European country is anchoring itself at the centre of the global semiconductor and artificial intelligence (AI) hardware supply chain as a laser powerhouse.
The latest sign of Lithuania’s growing footprint in the sector came last month, when laser maker Litilit launched a new factory in the capital, Vilnius. The company’s expansion unfolds as the Baltic nation of fewer than 3 million people navigates fraught ties with Beijing, which has targeted Lithuania with a slew of informal trade restrictions over its Taiwan stance.
In 2021, Lithuania emerged as a symbol of Taiwan’s expanding diplomatic ties in Europe by allowing the opening of a Taiwanese representative office in Vilnius, breaking the long-standing convention of using “Taipei” in the names of such offices.
Beijing responded sharply to the move, downgrading its diplomatic ties with Lithuania and imposing trade measures, including banning certain food and alcohol products from the country and blocking many Lithuanian exporters from the Chinese market.
Lithuania’s new government, which took office earlier this year, has pledged to normalise ties with Beijing, but frictions persist.
In June, Beijing said it hoped Lithuania would “take early and decisive action to correct its mistakes, return to the right track of adhering to the one-China principle and create conditions for the normalisation of China-Lithuania relations”.
One month later, the Chinese commerce ministry put Lithuanian laser manufacturer Ekspla on its European dual-use exports control list, alongside 13 other European Union entities.
Despite this setback, the Lithuanian laser industry continues to thrive. The new Litilit facility will be able to produce 3,000 femtosecond lasers per year at full capacity.
These advanced lasers can emit pulses of light so short that they can process materials, including glass, with high precision and minimal heat damage to surrounding structures. This ability has become crucial for chipmakers as they explore glass as a substrate for the next generation of AI infrastructure.
According to Litilit, the new factory boasts the world’s largest potential production capacity for this class of advanced laser. By comparison, global output stood at an estimated 9,600 units last year.
Litilit plans to produce an initial 1,000 units in its first year before scaling to full capacity, exporting most of its output to Europe, North America and Asia, according to the company.
Nikolajus Gavrilinas, Litilit’s co-founder and CEO, said the commercial reality of the supply chain often overrode political posturing.
“We are absolutely in line with Lithuanian official policies about US, Chinese, European and [other] political alignments,” Gavrilinas said.
“We are looking for strong partnerships with all three [regions]: Europe, the US and Asia. Together with our partners, we are ready to expand our capacity to tens of thousands of lasers per year.”
He said that while he had observed some tensions between European countries and China, if one side needed a technology and another needed a different technology, and they were part of each other’s global supply chain, they would not be easily stopped from doing business.
“Until it becomes a real resistance for each other, everything is very smooth,” he said.
Litilit CEO Nikolajus Gavrilinas says femtosecond technology is expected to be the industry standard for the next two decades. Photo: Litilit
While public discussion of AI often focuses on software and algorithms, Gavrilinas said the growth of AI data centres was driving demand for the advanced manufacturing tools – including femtosecond lasers – needed to build their hardware.
As AI models require more processing power, data centres are changing how their chips are assembled. One emerging approach, known as advanced packaging, stacks multiple chips on a single board.
A glass interposer is a thin piece of glass connecting chiplets and memory to the package substrate. The thermal expansion properties of glass can be tuned to closely match that of silicon, making it a promising material for advanced semiconductor packaging.
However, light from conventional lasers passes through transparent materials, making processing difficult. Femtosecond lasers overcome this by focusing energy directly within the glass to form precise pathways and internal structures.
Gavrilinas noted that femtosecond lasers could be used to carve electrical paths inside glass in a method similar to 3D printing, adding that this application “opens a lot of possibilities”. He said this process could also be used on other transparent materials, such as polycrystalline diamond, an ultra-hard material for cutting tools and metalworking dies.
“Imagine … glass coming back to the main domain of everything, like as a base plate for electronics, chips and so on. This is a fabulous transformation,” he said.
“Our goal is to move the femtosecond laser from niche applications to the commodity market,” Gavrilinas said, comparing its present growth phase to earlier lasers that moved from labs to everyday tasks such as engraving names on cups.
While legacy laser manufacturers rely heavily on manual assembly, typically producing around one laser per worker each year on average, the Litilit facility has achieved around 85 per cent automation with a workforce of around 100 to 200 people.
“When we have this level of automation … we can scale it up in any country, in any region,” Gavrilinas said, adding that this helped limit the effects of “turbulence” in labour costs or politics.
The company is evaluating further expansion with potential production hubs in Singapore, Japan, the United States and Europe to meet surging demand, according to Gavrilinas.
When asked about the potential for even faster attosecond lasers, Gavrilinas said femtosecond technology would be the industry standard for the next 20 years.
“For the next two decades, we believe that it will be femtosecond. Maybe later [when] we broaden our experience and everything around it, it may be attosecond,” he said.
The Baltic nation’s laser industry traces back to 1962, when Lithuania was still part of the Soviet Union and three students were sent to Moscow to study laser physics and quantum electronics. By 1966, the country’s first laser was fired at Vilnius University.
Today, Lithuania has more than 60 laser and photonics companies and over 2,000 specialists across its research centres, and the country exports more than 90 per cent of its laser output, according to Vilnius University.
Undeterred by the threat of blacklists, the Lithuanian laser sector has sought to work with experts from Taiwan, a global semiconductor powerhouse.
According to the Research Council of Lithuania, which provides research and development funding, 60 researchers from Lithuania and Taiwan explored new partnership opportunities and exchanged knowledge last month at a three-day laser technology workshop in the European nation.
The event was organised as part of a 2023 agreement between the research council and Taiwan’s National Science and Technology Council to fund joint research projects in biotechnology, laser and materials science, engineering and computer information technology.
